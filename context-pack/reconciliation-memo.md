# Reconciliation Memo — AI Engineering Factory

**From:** Framework review
**Re:** Reconciling the Strategy & Technical Blueprint (Word, v1.0) with the Technical Build Guide (deck, 19 slides), and siting the platform inside Amplify
**Status:** For decision. Nothing below is committed until the four decisions in §7 are taken.

---

## 1. What this memo is for

You have two documents that read as one plan and aren't. The Word blueprint and the deck disagree on the product, the architecture, the stack, the phase count and the timeline. Neither is wrong — each is internally coherent — but each was written for a different half of the work, and neither says so.

This memo does four things: names the two lanes explicitly, separates what they share from what they don't, resolves every contradiction between the two documents with a recommendation, and sites the whole thing inside the Amplify platform.

---

## 2. The two lanes

The programme covers two kinds of work. Almost every disagreement between the documents dissolves once this is stated.

**Lane A — Modernize.** Existing applications, many of them legacy, that need upgrading. The Word blueprint is written for this lane end to end: Capability A is Discovery & Reverse Engineering and the MVP journey is *existing app → understand → change → test → PR → deploy*.

**Lane B — Build.** New applications and products. The deck is written for this lane: Phase 1 is "describe an app in plain language and watch it get built."

They are not competing proposals. They are two entry points to one platform, and the documents should say so on their first page.

### Why one platform rather than two

The shared substrate is larger than the divergence:

| Shared — build once | Lane-specific — build twice |
|---|---|
| AI gateway and model routing | Context layer *content* (discovered vs. prescribed) |
| Secure execution sandbox | Sandbox *profile* (persistent index vs. ephemeral) |
| Orchestration state machine and human gates | Deployment target (inherited vs. mandated) |
| Change Record and audit trail | Maintainability baseline (relative vs. absolute) |
| Policy engine and data classification | Verification tier defaults (high vs. low blast radius) |
| CI/CD integration, test generation, code review | Entry point and first prompt |
| FinOps, observability, competency model | |

Seven shared components against five divergences, and every divergence is a configuration of a shared component rather than a separate system. One platform is the right call — the documents just need to admit there are two lanes running through it.

### The requirement neither document states

**Every application Lane B builds becomes a Lane A application.** Usually within eighteen months, and faster when it was AI-generated by someone who could not fully explain it.

If Lane B produces applications Lane A cannot understand, the platform manufactures its own future workload. The fix is cheap now and expensive later: **Lane B must emit the artifacts Lane A consumes** — knowledge-graph entries, current documentation, dependency records, decision records and real tests — from the first commit, not as a later documentation exercise.

This is the strongest technical argument for one platform rather than two, and it should be stated as an architecture principle, not left implicit.

Twelve, in descending order of consequence. The recommendation column is what I would defend in review.

| # | Item | Blueprint | Deck | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Vector store | Azure AI Search | Postgres + pgvector | **pgvector.** Amplify already runs Postgres 15 (§5); this removes a new service and the blueprint already names pgvector as its acceptable alternative. |

## 4. The competency layer, and why it has to come back

Both documents place human approval gates throughout. Neither says who stands at them or how that person became qualified.

That was tolerable when the gate approver was assumed to be an experienced engineer. It is not tolerable given the stated workforce premise: **people with very little software development experience, doing modernization work.**

Legacy modernization is the most judgment-intensive work in software — unfamiliar code, undocumented behaviour, unknowable blast radius, original authors gone. Pointing the least experienced people at it is defensible only if the verification machinery is explicit. As the documents stand, the gates are theatre: a junior approving a plan they cannot evaluate is a rubber stamp with an audit trail, which is worse than no gate because it manufactures evidence of scrutiny that did not occur.

**Recommendation: reinstate the competency ladder and verification tiers as a section of the blueprint** (not as a separate document — that is how it got lost).

### The lane routing that makes it work

The two lanes solve the staffing problem if people are routed between them deliberately:

- **Lane B is the right place for inexperienced people.** New application, no legacy behaviour to preserve, bounded consequences, standards enforceable from the first commit, no tribal knowledge required.
- **Lane A is where inexperienced people are most dangerous** — it requires precisely the judgment they have not yet built: knowing what might break somewhere they were not looking.

So: start in Lane B, build demonstrated judgment where being wrong is cheap, earn entry to Lane A. That gives the competency ladder a structural job rather than leaving it as generic policy, and it gives the honest answer to "a fresher performs like a senior": **senior throughput inside a bounded blast radius that widens as judgment is demonstrated.**

Three controls carry this and are worth defending if challenged:

1. **Explain-back.** Nobody advances a gate on work they cannot explain with the assistant closed. Cheap to enforce, and the only control that reliably catches someone shipping what they don't understand.
2. **Verification tier set at ticket creation, not at PR time.** Review intensity follows consequence-of-being-wrong, not effort or line count.
3. **Defect escape rate as the primary metric.** If it rises while velocity rises, the programme is failing regardless of how good adoption looks.

## 5. Siting the platform in Amplify

**Recommendation: yes — host the Factory's control plane as an Amplify application. Do not host its execution sandbox there.**

### Why Amplify is the right host

**There is already a precedent for exactly this shape.** Practice HUB is a Node/Express + React application living inside the repo with its own stack, its own auth, its own `ph_*` tables in the shared `amplify` database, and its own host ports outside the nginx routing. The Factory's orchestrator and agent services are naturally Node or Python rather than Django — Practice HUB is the pattern to copy.

**Several Factory capabilities already have homes.** Building them fresh would violate the platform's own reuse-before-build principle on its first day:

| Factory capability | Existing Amplify module | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Reusable Asset Marketplace | 08 Assets + Integration HUB Assets | Extend, don't rebuild |
| Tool register (governance §3.3) | 10 Toolchain | Extend |
| FinOps / cost attribution | 03 Value | Extend |
| AI agent monitoring | 07 Agents + AgentActivity | Extend — the model is already there |
| Governance stats and user access | 05 Admin | Extend |
| Competency ladder and training | 09 Training + Copilot Academy | Extend — the ladder is a course path |

That is six of the Factory's capabilities already partly built. The genuinely new services are the gateway, the orchestrator, the context/index service, the maintainability engine and the audit service.

**The infrastructure fits.** Postgres 15 takes pgvector as an extension, resolving contradiction #7 in the deck's favour. nginx already dispatches by route, so a new surface is a config block. Azure Pipelines already exists. The design system is defined and the Factory console inherits it.

### The one thing that must stay out

**The secure execution sandbox does not go in the compose stack.**

The sandbox exists to run untrusted, AI-generated code. Placing it on the same Docker network as the Postgres holding OPmobility's governance data means a sandbox escape or a prompt-injected agent reaches production data. The blueprint's §15 is emphatic on this and is right.

The split is clean and maps onto the blueprint's own layer diagram:

```
IN AMPLIFY (control plane)           OUTSIDE (data plane)
─────────────────────────            ────────────────────
Layer 1  Experience / console        Layer 6  Secure Execution Sandbox
Layer 2  Orchestration                        · separate network
Layer 3  Control plane / gateway              · separate host or subscription
Layer 4  Enterprise context (pgvector)        · no route to amplify Postgres
Layer 5  Agent definitions                    · reached over an API only
Layer 7  Toolchain integration
```

This is a decision to take now. Retrofitting isolation after the sandbox has convenient database access is a rewrite, and it is the finding that will end an internal security review.

### What in Amplify needs fixing first

The platform's own repository will be read by anyone evaluating a governance platform. Four things currently undercut the pitch:

1. **`makemigrations` runs on every container start.** This contradicts the blueprint's "everything version controlled" principle directly — migrations can differ between environments because they are generated at runtime rather than committed. Fix before the Factory ships, and it is a small fix.
2. **Credentials are hard-coded in compose** (`amplify/amplify123`, the pgAdmin password). Acceptable for a POC, indefensible once the same stack holds model-provider API keys. Move to an env file now, Key Vault before pilot.
3. **Two Integration Hub implementations exist side by side, not synced, with no canonical answer.** Your own CLAUDE.md says "confirm with the team which one is canonical before extending either."
4. **The `practicehub` Django app is an unrouted, unused `managed=False` mirror** of tables another service owns.

Items 3 and 4 are worth reframing. They are not embarrassments — they are **the exact failure mode the Factory exists to prevent, already present in your codebase, and documented.** Duplicated logic that has drifted, with no owner and no traceability. That makes them the single most persuasive demo material available to you (§6).

## 6. What this means for the showcase

Two acts, in this order.

**Act one — the controlled demo.** A synthetic legacy expense-claims application, built for the purpose, with six business rules deliberately buried in nested conditionals and an operator answer key. Four of the six are undocumented and genuinely easy to miss. The demo runs the same task twice: once with a naive prompt, once through the governed pipeline with the context pack loaded. The naive run drops rules the governed run finds and documents. Repeatable, safe, and you know in advance exactly what the agent will surface.

**Act two — the real codebase.** Point the same Discovery capability at the Amplify repo and let it find the Integration Hub duplication. This is far more convincing than any synthetic artifact, because the room recognises the code, and it demonstrates the capability on a problem the organisation already knows it has.

Act one earns the credibility to run act two. Running act two alone risks a live failure on real code in front of leadership; running act one alone invites "yes, but that was a toy."

## 7. Decisions needed

Four. Everything else follows from them.

| # | Decision | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Are Lane A and Lane B both in scope, stated explicitly in both documents? | **Yes** |
| 2 | Does the sandbox sit outside the Amplify compose stack? | **Yes — non-negotiable** |
| 3 | Does the competency ladder come back as a blueprint section? | **Yes** |
| 4 | Which lane goes first? | **Lane B** — lower risk, faster to a working demo, correct fit for an inexperienced workforce, and it proves the governance machinery before you point it at systems that can hurt you |

Decision 4 contradicts the blueprint's current MVP choice, which selects a real existing application. It is nonetheless consistent with the blueprint's own stated principle of staging by risk: a new application has lower blast radius than a change to a live one, even though "build" sounds more advanced than "understand."

## 8. Still open

These are unanswered and gate Lane A specifically. Lane B can proceed without them.

1. **What does "upgrade" mean concretely** — framework version uplift, re-platform, rewrite, or re-host? These are four unrelated programmes and the architecture differs for each.
2. **What are the legacy technologies?** Model competence and parser availability vary enormously between COBOL, VB6, Oracle Forms, .NET Framework and Java 8. Some make the blueprint's approach straightforwardly workable; others make it research.
3. **Are the legacy applications in Git, with tests, and reproducibly buildable?** The blueprint's MVP states these as prerequisites rather than risks. Legacy estates routinely have none of them, and if yours don't, there is a preliminary phase neither document contains.
4. **Internal capability or client offering?** Still undecided, and it now matters more: client legacy estates cannot be centrally indexed the way this architecture assumes.
